# Teaching statement, Ronak Vyas

Draft for review, to be tailored to each institution before use

Good AI teaching starts where the learner already works. In my own training sessions for business teams adopting generative AI, the sessions that landed were never the ones that opened with model architecture. They opened with a real task from the learner's week, a report to draft, a customer question to answer, a process that eats an afternoon, and built the concepts around that task.

That shapes how I would teach in a classroom. Each topic pairs a short concept briefing with a worked example from industry practice, followed by hands on time where students apply the idea to a case they choose. Assessment follows the same pattern, favouring applied projects, use case evaluations and short reflective memos over memorisation, because the skill employers ask for is judgement about AI, not recall of definitions.

I also teach limits as a first class topic. Students learn to check AI output, to recognise when a use case should not be automated, and to understand the governance, privacy and risk questions that decide whether an AI idea survives in a real organisation. That balance of enthusiasm and scepticism is the habit I most want learners to leave with, whether they are degree students, working professionals in continuing education, or community learners meeting these tools for the first time.
